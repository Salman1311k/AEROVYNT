Personal baseline vs lab range

Do personal baselines see changes that population ranges miss?

A post-flight draw counts as a personal change when it moves more than the 95% reference change value from that person’s pre-flight mean. Any such rule flags some draws by chance, so it was also run on 20,000 simulated studies with no real change.

Evidence ladder
  1. 1 · Simulation benchmarksReached. 120 crews per scenario, five scenarios, stress tests on fresh seeds. Shows trade-offs under assumptions we defined.
  2. 2 · Real wearable noise checkReached. 13 people from PMData (no illness labels) used to calibrate noise and bound false alarms.
  3. 3 · Real NASA lab dataReached, with a caveat. Inspiration4 OSD-575: 4 crew, 3-day flight. Shows the mechanism; cannot settle detection.

Clinician and expert feedback
Limitations
1
No validation on real illness labels. Real data is unlabelled (PMData) or 4 crew over 3 days (OSD-575). Detection is shown only on simulated events.
2
Simulated benchmarks. Event shapes are ours: rates show trade-offs, not real-world accuracy. Slow 25–45 day cycles remain a weakness. The stress indicator was added after the benchmark and is not part of it.
3
Thresholds were set on the simulator. The engine’s limits were frozen using the same simulator that produces the benchmark, so benchmark detection rates are optimistic. The simulator’s sleep noise (0.35 h) is lower than real wearable users (median 0.93 h); a stress test that resamples real PMData sleep noise is included, and detection there is 30–99% by scenario (43% for subtle, 30% for slow drift). With slow 25–45 day cycles, or all stressors at once, AEROVYNT action false alarms rise to about 35–40% and the plain personal z-score (about 11%) is better.
3b
Illustrative thresholds. Limits follow common clinical conventions; the fast/slow clock rule is a design choice, not clinically tested.
4
Not a medical device. It never replaces the Flight Surgeon. Demo days 1–28 are simulated; entries are processed locally.
